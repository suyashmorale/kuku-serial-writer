# Six Twelve

“When did you last see Aadit’s dark coat?”

The kitchen clock clicked to 6:11. Inspector Bhuvan Negi laid a photograph beside Meher’s untouched toast, careful not to set it in the smear of pickle.

The drying shed at Bagh Number Seven was black inside. A body lay beneath its fallen rafters. Meher recognised the coat by its horn buttons; she had sewn the top one back on last week. On one hand, a gold ring caught the photographer’s flash.

“Yesterday afternoon,” she said. “The coat was on the veranda hook.”

“He wore it when he left?” Bhuvan asked.

“I didn’t say that.”

Shanta stood behind Meher’s chair, both hands resting on its back. “That is my son.”

“The coat is his. The ring appears to be his.” Bhuvan turned the photograph face down. “The identification is provisional.”

“You expect me to thank you for the distinction?” Shanta asked.

“No, ji. I expect the person who died to be identified properly.”

Meher wanted him to take the photograph away. Instead, he took a peppermint from his uniform pocket and left it wrapped between his fingers.

Shanta poured tea for him. Aadit’s blue cup still stood beside the kettle, with a chip Meher’s thumbnail always found. Shanta lifted it and put it behind the sugar tin before she sat down.

Meher reached for the crime paperback tucked beside her chair. Its cover showed a bride holding a pair of garden shears; a pencil stuck out from the spine. Beyond it, the hook by the kitchen door was bare. Her red wool shawl usually hung there. She tried to remember taking it upstairs and could not.

“Who could have taken things from the house last night?” Bhuvan asked.

“Arre, inspector,” Shanta said. “We don’t lock cupboards against one another.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

Meher kept her hand on the paperback. The coat had been on one hook. Her shawl had been on another. She said nothing about either hook.

At 6:12, her phone rang. The number on the screen was unfamiliar.

Bhuvan looked at it. Meher answered.

“It’s me.”

The man took a short, impatient breath. “Meher.”

Aadit did that when he wanted her to stop asking a question before she had finished it. Her fingers tightened around the phone.

“Where are you?”

“Not with the police. Not yet.”

“I’m sitting with Inspector Negi. I can give him the phone.”

“No.”

Bhuvan held out his hand. Meher turned away from it. “Then tell me something only you know.”

For a moment she heard a vehicle engine and, farther off, a horn. Then the man said, “Please, Meher.”

It sounded exactly like Aadit asking her to trust him. It proved nothing.

“Why did you let me sit here,” she asked, “while he showed me a photograph of your body?”

The engine grew louder. “I knew they’d suspect you.”

“Then why didn’t you come home?”

The call ended.

Meher kept the phone against her ear until Bhuvan said her name. Then she turned the screen toward him: the number, the time, the length of the call. She repeated every word, including the warning about the police.

Shanta looked at the phone but did not touch it. “Someone could have made a cruel joke.”

“Yes,” Meher said.

Bhuvan wrote down the number. Meher watched him put a question mark beside it, not Aadit’s name.

If the voice belonged to her husband, he had known they would suspect her. He had waited until morning to call.

# The Answer She Gives

“Start with when you came home.”

Bhuvan slid a sheet of paper across the kitchen table. The clock had reached 6:16. Meher set her phone beside it, the unfamiliar number still on the screen.

“You heard him,” she said.

“I heard what you told me he said.”

“He used my name.”

“So did I.” Bhuvan put his pen next to the paper. “Write what you remember from last night.”

Meher took the pencil from the spine of her paperback. The bride on its cover held her garden shears at an angle that would have made them useless. Meher turned the book face down and wrote: *Returned from Lower Bazaar at 9:15. Went upstairs at 9:20 and stayed in my room reading until 10:00.*

“There,” she said.

“What fixes nine fifteen?”

“I came straight home.”

“That fixes the route.”

She looked at the line. It was the sort of time she might have checked on the kitchen clock. She could picture the clock clearly, including the grease spot above the six. She could not picture looking at it last night.

“And nine twenty?” Bhuvan asked.

“My book was upstairs.”

“It’s here now.”

“I brought it down this morning. The pencil was in the spine.”

“Was it in the spine at nine twenty?”

Meher pressed the point harder against the paper. “Do you want me to say approximately before every word?”

“Only the approximate ones.”

Beyond the kitchen door, the veranda hook stood empty. Her shawl was still missing from the hook beside it. She kept the pencil where it was.

“Trace the number,” she said. “Then you can ask Aadit where I was.”

“I will trace it. A borrowed phone tells me whose handset carried a call, not who spoke into it.” Bhuvan turned his peppermint over in its wrapper. “My nephew Manu borrowed mine once to call his sister. Tara had stopped answering his number. He thought she might answer mine.”

“Did she?”

“Once. Then she stopped answering mine too.” He put the peppermint away. “Read me the second line.”

Shanta stood beside the sugar tin, one hand on its lid. She had not touched her tea.

Meher read her statement aloud. Bhuvan repeated it back, slower. “You remember coming from the bazaar. You haven’t fixed the time. You remember the book. You haven’t fixed when you read it upstairs. Did you see Aadit leave?”

“No.”

“Anyone take his coat?”

“No.”

Shanta’s thumb moved across the tin lid. She said nothing about the coat, or the family’s habit of leaving things on hooks for anyone to take.

“Then that’s what we have,” Bhuvan said.

“At nine forty,” Shanta said, “I took a hot-water bottle to her room.”

Meher looked up. “You didn’t mention that.”

“The light was off. I opened the door and put the bottle on the bed. You weren’t there.”

“Did you see my face at nine forty?”

“I saw an empty room. The landing clock showed nine forty.”

Meher could remember carrying the paperback upstairs. She could remember filling a glass at the kitchen tap. She could not place either at 9:40.

Bhuvan drew a line through *stayed in my room reading until 10:00*. “Begin that part again.”

Shanta had waited until Meher put her certainty on paper to say the room was empty.

# The Driver’s Number

Naina took Meher’s phone off the kitchen table before Bhuvan could ask where she had been at 9:40.

“Arre, we can argue about her bedroom after we call this.” She tapped the 6:12 number and put it on speaker.

A bus horn blared through the phone. Then a man said, “Yes, the morning service is running. You’re the fourth person to ask.”

“We didn’t ask,” Naina said.

“Then you’re my favourite.”

Bhuvan leaned toward the phone. “Inspector Bhuvan Negi. Who am I speaking to?”

“Jitendra Rawat. Whose bus have I hit?”

“No one’s, as far as I know. Is this your phone?”

Jitendra said it was. At Do-Mile Bend that morning, a man had borrowed it while Jitendra watched another bus reverse between two parked taxis. The man returned the phone just after 6:12.

Meher gripped the edge of the table. A real bus driver. A real phone. She wanted to thank him, though he had done nothing for her except lend something to a stranger.

“Keep your call log,” Bhuvan said. “I’ll come and speak to you.”

“You can buy a ticket like everyone else.”

“Can you describe the man?”

“Over this line? I was watching a bus reverse, inspector, not conducting an identification parade.”

The horn sounded again, farther off. Bhuvan wrote *Jitendra Rawat* beneath Meher’s crossed-out account. He did not cross anything else out.

“People lend phones,” he said.

“Manu borrowed my only charging cable,” Naina said. “At the counter. Lost it the same day.”

Meher looked at her. Naina’s thumb rubbed a crack in the table varnish.

“He never paid the hundred and eighty for the new one. Then my shutter jammed, and he stood there holding it up for an hour while I finished Mrs. Kapur’s screen.” Naina looked up and met Meher’s eyes. “Never mind.”

Bhuvan capped his pen. Meher pushed her statement toward him. “You have the driver’s name. Strike out the part where I’m suspected of murdering my husband.”

“I haven’t written that you murdered him.”

“You crossed out the part where I didn’t.”

“I crossed out a time you couldn’t establish.” He tapped the number. “This is a lead I can check. It doesn’t tell me who spoke, who wore Aadit’s coat, or where you were last night.”

“He’s more than she had ten minutes ago,” Naina said.

He was. Meher hated that a man who sounded like Aadit had given her a witness instead of an answer.

“I’ll meet you at the bend,” Bhuvan told Jitendra.

“Anardana Dhaba. Ask for the bench that doesn’t get dripped on.” Jitendra paused. “The fellow sat there with my phone. When he got up, something fell out of his pocket. I picked it up and put it under the dry end so the tea boys wouldn’t sweep it away.”

“What?”

“A handkerchief. It’s still here.”

“Don’t touch it again,” Bhuvan said.

“Wasn’t planning to,” Jitendra replied. “Not with those dark red spots on it.”

# The Driver’s View

“Tell him what the man said before you handed over your phone,” Meher told Jitendra Rawat.

At Anardana Dhaba, Jitendra stood beside the bench with one eye on his parked bus. “He said he needed to call Meher Sood.”

Meher turned to Bhuvan. “By name.”

“By name,” Jitendra agreed. “Now don’t ask me to swear he was your husband. The driver behind me was reversing into a taxi.”

“He missed it,” Tara said from the dhaba doorway.

“By the width of my moustache. Which you could try appreciating, Tara ji.”

Bhuvan crouched at the dry end of the bench. The handkerchief lay tucked beneath it, white cotton marked with three dark red spots. He photographed it from two angles, then slipped it into a bag without unfolding it.

“Was he bleeding?” Meher asked.

“Someone left a handkerchief,” Bhuvan said.

“Someone who knew my name.”

“Possibly.”

Meher looked toward the road. A real call from a real borrowed phone, and Bhuvan still spoke as if the man might have picked her out of a directory. At Cedar Terrace, they had a photograph of a coat and a ring. Nobody had yet shown her a face.

Tara came down the dhaba’s two concrete steps. “Where was he sitting?”

Jitendra pointed to the bench. “Dry end. Your roof still drips over the other one.”

“It dripped over that one last monsoon.” Tara looked up at a square of tin held down by a brick. “Manu patched it for me with a piece of his own rain cover. Then he sold my spare gas cylinder while I was at the wholesaler.”

Naina, beside Meher, stopped rubbing her thumbnail against her phone case.

“He promised to buy it back,” Tara said.

“Did he?” Jitendra asked.

“The roof stopped leaking.”

A drop struck the far end of the bench. Jitendra moved his foot.

“Which way did the man go?” Tara asked.

“Toward the bus road. I’ve told the inspector on the phone: I didn’t get a look worth giving him.”

“Then give him what you did see,” Tara said. “Not what kind of man you think borrows a phone.”

Jitendra bristled, but stepped away from the bench to show Bhuvan where he had stood. He faced the two taxis, raised his hands to mark the reversing bus, and nearly backed into a tea crate.

“Here,” Tara said.

She was looking at the bench. Between two seat planks, a corner of paper showed beneath a splinter. Bhuvan photographed it before easing it free with gloved fingers.

“A public bench,” he said as he held it flat. “This could have been here since yesterday.”

“Let me see,” Meher said.

“First you hear that part.”

“I heard it.”

It was a grocery slip from Bansi Provisions in Lower Bazaar, dated 8:47 the previous evening. Sugar. Stove wicks. Under *delivery*, someone had written *Cedar Terrace*.

Beneath the address, in darker ink, were two more words: *Meher Sood*. The stranger could have read her name here before he ever borrowed the phone.

# What the Ring Proves

“When can we bring him home?” Raghav asked.

He stood beneath the pickle jars on Cedar Terrace’s veranda, holding a sheet of paper with the funeral wood merchant’s rates written down the side. Meher had barely taken off her shoes. Bhuvan was still knocking road dust from his trousers.

Shanta set a glass of water before the inspector. “First, his ring. I can clean it.”

“The ring stays with the body,” Bhuvan said. “So does the coat. They’re evidence.”

“They are Aadit’s.”

“That tells me who owned them. Not who was wearing them.”

Raghav folded the paper once, precisely. “My brother did not lend his coat.”

“You heard from the driver,” Meher said. “A man called me. He knew my name.”

“And you say it was Aadit.”

“I said he sounded like Aadit.” She hated the correction even as she made it. “My name was on a grocery slip under the bench. I don’t know whether he saw it.”

Raghav looked at her. “Are you questioning who died, or fixing where you were last night?”

“Both would be convenient for her,” Shanta said softly. She picked up Meher’s untouched glass and moved it clear of the veranda edge. “Her room was empty when I went there at nine-forty.”

Meher remembered the line Bhuvan had put through her written account. She could not draw it back with an argument about a phone call.

“People borrow coats,” Bhuvan said.

“Not that one,” Raghav replied.

“My nephew Manu borrowed my jacket once without asking. Sewed a button back on before I noticed.” Bhuvan took a peppermint from his pocket but did not unwrap it. “Kept the fifty rupees I’d left inside. Said I should thank him for the stitching.”

Despite herself, Meher pictured Manu at somebody’s kitchen table, threading a needle through a stranger’s button. Tara had said he patched her leaking roof, then sold her gas cylinder. Neither story told Meher whose coat lay in the shed.

“I have to get back to the sorting shed,” Raghav said. “Tell me what I need to sign to bring Aadit home.”

“At the station,” Bhuvan said. “And not yet.”

By the time Meher reached Bhuvan’s desk, a constable was trying to flatten a curled evidence label beneath a brass paperweight. She asked Bhuvan to examine the body before anybody signed for it. He pulled a blank statement form from a drawer.

“Tell me what you recognized in the photograph,” he said.

“The coat. Aadit’s brown one, with the torn lining near the cuff.”

“And the ring?”

“It looked like his wedding ring.”

“The face?”

She looked at the ruled lines on the form. “No.”

Bhuvan wrote that down. He said he could request the examination himself, and that the identification would remain provisional. Then he turned the page for her to read. Meher checked every sentence before she signed.

Raghav came in carrying the folded sheet from the veranda. He had written a request to claim the body on its clean side. Bhuvan laid it beside Meher’s statement.

“I won’t authorize release before the examination,” he said.

Raghav looked from one page to the other. His finger stopped beside Meher’s signature. “You can tell Ma yourself why you won’t let us bury Aadit.”

# Tara’s Cup

“Jitendra leaves his cups wherever the bus stops,” Tara said. “If you’ve come to charge him for it, get in line.”

She lifted a chipped glass from the bench outside Anardana Dhaba. Meher had come down from the police station with her signed statement still folded in her handbag. The bench was where Jitendra said a man had borrowed his phone at 6:12.

“I came to ask about the man.”

“So did the inspector.”

“He said he was Aadit when he called me.” Meher watched Tara tip cold tea into a bucket. “You knew Aadit before I did. If I told you how he sounded—”

“I’d have your description of a voice.” Tara set the glass upside down on a tray. “I didn’t see his face. I didn’t hear the call.”

A bus sounded three short blasts from the bend. Tara glanced at the road before reaching for the kettle. Meher had seen her recognize a driver that way, without looking up. For one foolish second, she wanted to ask whether Tara could do the same with a husband.

“He asked for me by name,” Meher said.

Tara tapped the plank beneath the bench with her sandal. “Your name was on a grocery slip under here. Negi took it.”

“He might not have seen it.”

“And you might be right.” Tara poured tea into two cups, then a third. She stopped with the kettle in her hand.

The third cup sat at the empty end of the table.

“Has he called the police?” Tara asked.

“No. He wouldn’t speak to Bhuvan when he called.”

“Achha.” Tara put down the kettle. “Why not?”

“He said he knew they’d suspect me.”

“That answers why *you* needed him to call. I asked why he wouldn’t speak to them.”

Meher had no answer that did not come from Aadit’s mouth. She pushed aside the cup Tara had placed before her.

Tara drew out a chair, but did not sit. “Eight years ago, I came out of the hospital after they told me my baby had died. Manu was waiting by the gate with tea in a paper cup. My hands were shaking so badly he had to hold it for me.”

Meher looked up. She knew Manu was Tara’s brother; she had not known about the baby.

“He drank the tea cold while I stood there. The same month he took six hundred rupees from my till without asking.” Tara rubbed a thumb over a mark the glass had left on the tray. “Don’t make me choose which of those happened.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“I went home because everyone who told me my baby was dead expected me to go home.”

“Was Aadit one of them?”

Tara pulled out the chair and pushed it back into place. “You came for a voice, ji. Don’t ask me to give you our whole marriage’s worth of silence.”

“You and Aadit were never married.”

“No,” Tara said. “He could leave a conversation unfinished without a wedding.”

Meher took out her phone. “If he calls again, I can ring you.”

Tara slid the phone back across the table. She picked up the untouched third cup and emptied it into the bucket.

“Call your inspector. If that is Aadit, he can tell me himself why he left his wife to answer questions about his corpse.”

Meher left without asking Tara to listen to the voice after all. If the caller was Aadit, he had known she was under suspicion—and had let her sign a statement about his body instead of speaking to Bhuvan himself.

# The Last Bus Out

“Did the man who borrowed your phone leave town on your bus?”

Jitendra Rawat dragged a wet rag across his windscreen. Mud from the Do-Mile road ran down the glass and onto Meher’s sandal.

“Good morning to you too.”

Behind him, the Lower Bazaar departure board advertised a 10:45 run to the district hospital. Naina checked her phone: 10:32.

“You said he was at Tara’s bench at 6:12,” Meher said. “Where did he go after?”

“Am I a ticket register with legs?” Jitendra wrung out the rag. “Ask about my bus. I’ll answer about my bus.”

Naina held up his ticket pad. “Your first stop was Do-Mile Bend?”

“Six twenty. Then Lower Bazaar, six thirty-eight. The timings are on the board, if nobody’s rubbed them off.”

“Where could you see the bench from?” Meher asked.

Jitendra climbed into the driver’s seat and beckoned her to the door. “From here while we were stopped. From this window when I pulled out.” He tapped the open glass. “The man was still outside at six twenty, standing by Tara’s water drum. He didn’t get on.”

“Did you see his face?”

“I saw a man who kept his head down while a bus full of people shouted for me to start it.” Jitendra looked at her. “No, I cannot give you his nose.”

Naina checked the departure board against the pencilled entries in his pad. “There were later buses. And he could have walked uphill.”

“Or stayed for breakfast,” Jitendra said. “Tara makes decent parathas when she isn’t calling my horn a public nuisance.”

They crossed the stand to let a bus reverse out. Beneath a closed rain-cover shop, Naina stopped. Its blue shutter had three pale squares where old posters had been scraped away.

“Manu waited there for an hour once,” she said. “Those tourists from the Chandigarh coach wouldn’t stop following me. He walked me up to my counter.”

Meher waited. Naina worried a loose corner of poster with her thumbnail.

“I’d lent him my old phone to repair. He sold it. For weeks he kept saying the replacement screen was coming.” She gave the shutter a short, angry glance. “I still let him walk me home.”

Jitendra came around the bus with his bucket. Naina stepped away from the shutter.

Her phone rang before they reached the road toward Cedar Terrace. Kavya’s name filled the screen.

“Did you find Aadit?” Kavya asked when Naina answered.

“We found Jitendra.”

“Come home, then. I’ll put tea on. Tell me what he said before you tell anyone else.”

“Achha, we’re—” Naina caught Meher’s look.

“Driver sahib,” Meher called, “the time again? At the dhaba?”

“Six twenty!” Jitendra called back. “Write it on your hand if you must.”

Naina pulled a grease-marked receipt from her pocket and wrote it down. “I’ll send Bhuvan the time myself,” she told Kavya.

There was a pause. “The tea will be ready,” Kavya said, and ended the call.

Naina began a message as they climbed. Meher read over her shoulder: *Aadit did not board the 6:20.*

“Not Aadit,” Meher said. “The man who used the phone.”

Naina deleted the name and sent the narrower account. The message showed delivered.

“If he calls again,” Meher said, “I’m putting Bhuvan on while I’m still on the line. He can hang up on both of us.”

# At the Gate

“Does Naina’s message mean you’ll release my brother?”

Raghav stood across the entrance to Cedar Terrace with the gate latch in his hand. Behind him, pickle jars crowded the veranda. Meher had climbed seventy-three steps to avoid an audience at the bus road, only to find one waiting at home.

“It means the man who borrowed Rawat’s phone didn’t board his six-twenty bus,” Bhuvan said. He had come up just behind Meher and Naina. “Nothing more.”

Raghav looked at Naina. “You couldn’t have written that plainly?”

“I did,” she said.

Someone called Meher’s name from the road.

Aadit came around the bend carrying his muddy shoes by their laces. His socks were wet. There was a scrape across his jaw he hadn’t had when she last saw him.

Meher knew him before he reached the gate. His face was not the face in the photograph. That should have made everything simple.

Shanta pushed past Raghav and caught Aadit’s face between her palms. “Come inside. Your feet are soaked.”

Raghav gripped his brother’s shoulder. Aadit winced, and Raghav let go as though he had meant only to brush off the dust.

“Where were you?” Naina asked.

“Let him put his shoes on first,” Shanta said.

Keshav slipped between the adults, holding a school notice folded into quarters. “Chachu, can you still sign my picnic slip? Papa said he would, but he wrote on the wrong line.”

“Yes,” Aadit said. “Give me a minute.”

Kavya put a hand on Keshav’s back. “Come and find your pencil, then. The one with the red top.” She led him through the front door before anyone could ask Aadit another question.

Bhuvan watched the door close. “Mr Sood. Did you use Jitendra Rawat’s phone at Do-Mile Bend this morning?”

“Yes.”

“To call your wife?”

“Yes.”

Raghav looked from one brother to the other. “Then I’ll withdraw the request.”

“You’ll have to,” Bhuvan said. “Your brother is standing here. The man at the shed is not Aadit Sood. We still have to examine him and find out who he is.”

The door opened a hand’s width. Keshav’s eyes went straight to Bhuvan’s uniform pocket.

“You still have those?”

Bhuvan pulled out a peppermint. “Manu used to take three when I offered him one. He’d leave the third on Tara’s till, as if that made the other two free.”

Keshav accepted the sweet. Bhuvan glanced at Kavya, visible behind him. “Keep him inside while we talk, please.”

“Of course,” she said, and shut the door gently.

Aadit reached for Meher. She took his hand. His fingers were cold, and for a moment she held them tight enough to hurt them both.

“At six-twelve,” she said, “you could talk to me.”

“I needed to know you were safe.”

“Bhuvan was beside me. He was asking why a body was wearing what looked like your ring.”

Aadit’s grip changed. “Meher—”

“You said you knew they would suspect me. So you knew what you were leaving me with.”

He looked toward the veranda. “Can we speak inside?”

“Yes,” Meher said, and let go of his hand. “Bhuvan can hear you too.”

Aadit opened the gate for her. Meher stayed beside the inspector.

# The Paperback

“Where did you spend the night?” Bhuvan asked.

Aadit stood at Cedar Terrace’s dining table in damp socks. His muddy shoes waited outside, and the scrape on his jaw had begun to dry dark at the edges.

“I went to the shed at Bagh Number Seven. I left before the fire. Afterward, I stayed in the orchard pump room until morning.”

“Why the pump room?”

Aadit glanced at Meher. “I didn’t want to go home.”

Bhuvan wrote *pump room* beneath *shed*. “I’ll check it. Who were you meeting?”

Meher waited. When Aadit said nothing, she asked, “Who?”

“I’ll tell the inspector privately.”

“At six-sixteen this morning,” she said, “he asked me where I was last night in front of your mother, your brother and anyone on the veranda with working ears.”

“I know.”

“You weren’t there.”

Naina lifted Keshav’s picnic slip out of the way of Bhuvan’s notebook. A stack of ₹40 paperbacks went with it, their covers bent from too many hands. The top book had a woman in a red sari watching flames through a window.

Meher knew the cover. She had bought it in Lower Bazaar last evening, though she’d written what was inside it months ago under the name R. M. Vyas. In chapter eleven, a wife arranged a fire so the town would believe her husband dead.

She slid the paperback beneath the dining bench.

Naina saw. She set the other books beside the pickle jar without a word.

Bhuvan tapped his pen against the notebook. “You knew Meher was being questioned. You said so on the phone.”

“I needed to know she was safe,” Aadit said.

“So you called her.”

“Yes.”

“But you didn’t call during the night. You didn’t come home at first light.” Bhuvan looked at the scrape on Aadit’s jaw, then back at his face. “What kept you in that room?”

Aadit rubbed his thumb against the bare place where his wedding ring should have been. “I needed time.”

“Manu once asked me not to mention the bus fare I’d given him,” Bhuvan said. “Promised he’d tell Tara himself.” He put the cap on his pen. “She heard it from me instead.”

Aadit looked up. For a moment Meher thought he might answer. Then he said, “I’ll show you the pump room.”

“That would help. The name of the person you met would help more.”

Aadit said nothing.

Bhuvan turned to Meher. “What took you to Lower Bazaar yesterday evening?”

“The bookrack. I came back around nine-fifteen and went upstairs about nine-twenty.” She kept her hands flat on the table. “Those times aren’t certain. I told you that when I signed.”

Keshav’s picnic slip fluttered off the table. Naina crouched to retrieve it from beneath the bench and came up with the paperback too. Meher took the book before Naina could turn it over.

Bhuvan closed his notebook. “When I check your account, bring me what you bought. A receipt, if you have one.”

“I’ll look,” Meher said.

“I can find it for you,” Aadit offered.

“That’s all right. I can do it myself.”

Bhuvan went out to check the pump room, Aadit following him. Meher stayed by the table, holding her purchase. On its back cover, beneath her borrowed name, a wife set a fire to make the police believe her husband was dead.

# The Tin

By the time Bhuvan and Aadit returned from the orchard pump room, Shanta had made fresh rotis. Bhuvan brought mud onto the veranda and said nothing about what he had found.

Raghav was at the dining table with the sorting ledger open beside his plate. “The trucks come at two. I need you on the accounts before then.”

Aadit sat. Shanta took the first roti off the tawa and put it on his plate, though Raghav’s hand was already out.

“Eat,” she told Aadit.

Meher set her paperback facedown on the bench, its pencil still wedged in the spine. Aadit’s scraped jaw was dark at the edges. He hadn’t touched the roti.

“The trucks can wait for an invoice,” she said. “The man at the shed can’t tell us why he was wearing Aadit’s coat.”

“Meher.” Shanta reached for the butter tin. “Don’t quarrel over breakfast.”

“It’s nearly lunch.”

“Then you’ve had all morning to begin.”

Raghav shut the ledger on his thumb. “The coat hung on the veranda hook. Anyone in this house could have taken it.”

“Who used that entrance last night?” Meher asked.

Shanta slid the butter towards Aadit. “Kavya brought in Keshav’s uniform. I went upstairs with the hot-water bottle. Raghav—”

“I can say where I was, Ma.”

The butter tin stopped beneath Shanta’s fingers. She looked at Raghav for a moment, then took her hand away.

Bhuvan pulled out a chair but did not sit. He set a peppermint beside his notebook. Once, Manu had driven a picker with a badly cut hand to the clinic, then charged him ₹60 for petrol before taking him home. Bhuvan kept the memory to himself. It told him what Manu might do for a person, and what he might take afterward. It told him nothing about the burned man.

“The examination will establish what it can,” he said. “A coat won’t give him a name.”

Meher looked from the veranda door to Aadit. A person could have lifted the coat off its hook without going farther into the house. She could check who had come through that door. For now, the only person who had described Aadit’s departure was Aadit.

“Did you wear your coat to the shed?”

“No.”

Raghav opened the ledger again, but his pencil stayed still.

Meher looked at Aadit’s bare left hand. “Where was your ring when you left?”

Aadit glanced at Bhuvan. “Can we speak upstairs?”

“At six-twelve you called me because you knew they’d suspect me. You can answer down here.”

“I’m not asking you to lie.”

“You didn’t have to. I told them I recognized your ring in the photograph.”

Raghav let the ledger fall shut. Shanta lifted Aadit’s roti, found it cold, and put it back without a word.

Meher had searched for a living husband while everyone argued over a dead one. Nobody had shown her where Aadit kept his ring.

Aadit looked past her at the kitchen doorway. “I left my wedding ring in the blue biscuit tin above the kitchen stove before I went to the shed.”

# The Tin on the Shelf

Shanta put Aadit’s untouched roti back on the tawa. Beside her, Raghav spread an invoice across the dining table.

“Thirty-six crates,” he told Aadit. “Sign before the two-o’clock trucks come.”

Naina reached for the last fresh roti. Shanta lifted it first and placed it on Aadit’s plate, beside the one she was reheating.

“He has one,” Naina said.

“He hasn’t eaten it.”

“Neither have I.”

Meher pushed the invoice away from the butter dish. “Aadit. Show Bhuvan the tin.”

Raghav took his unsigned invoice to the veranda and called the truck driver. Aadit led them into the kitchen, where the blue biscuit tin sat above the stove, half-hidden behind a jar of dried chillies. Its lid showed a faded picture of a smiling child holding a cream biscuit.

“I put the ring in there before I went to the shed,” Aadit said.

Bhuvan took out his phone. “Leave it where it is.”

He photographed the shelf, the jar, the tin. Shanta leaned past him to turn off the tawa.

“I moved those chillies yesterday evening,” she said. “They were too close to the heat.”

“At quarter past nine they were beside the flour,” Naina said. “I came in to charge a customer’s phone.”

“I put them back before nine.”

“You put *something* back. I had to move the flour to reach the socket.”

Shanta wiped her hands on the end of her sari. “Your phone was charging. Perhaps you weren’t watching my jars.”

“You also said Meher wasn’t in her room at nine-forty,” Naina said. “That one you watched carefully.”

Meher felt the invitation in it. She could have borrowed Naina’s certainty, fitted her own missing minutes around it. She kept quiet. Naina had seen a jar, not Meher.

Shanta’s mouth tightened. “You told me Manu would return that phone you lent him.”

“He sold it. I remember.” Naina wound a charging cable around her fingers. “I remember him walking me to my counter when two tourists followed me down Lower Bazaar, too. You don’t have to like him for me to know where the flour was.”

Bhuvan photographed the shelf again after Shanta stepped clear. “Who’s opened the tin since Aadit left?”

No one answered. Shanta said she had moved the chillies, not the tin. Naina had plugged in a phone. Aadit repeated that he had left his ring inside.

Bhuvan noted their answers, then took the tin down with gloved hands. The lid gave with a soft pop. Inside lay a broken biscuit and a dusting of sugar. No ring.

Aadit looked at Meher. “You saw it on the body.”

“I saw what looked like your ring in a photograph.”

“It’s the same ring.”

“I can’t tell you how it got there.”

Bhuvan turned the lid to photograph its inside. Meher caught his wrist before he closed it.

In the thin grime beneath the lid were two clean circles, side by side, each about the size of a ring.

“You said you left one,” she told Aadit.

“I did.”

“A second mark doesn’t prove what made it.” She looked at Bhuvan. “Please photograph both before you pack it.”

Aadit’s fingers had been resting against hers on the shelf. He took his hand away before the camera clicked.

# The Wearer’s Bones

Shanta set a hot plate of rajma at Aadit’s place. Meher’s dal had formed a skin.

Aadit picked up his plate and brought it to the empty chair beside hers. Meher slid it back across the oilcloth.

“It’s lunch,” he said.

“You missed breakfast too.”

Shanta reached for Meher’s plate. “I’ll warm yours, beti.”

“Leave it.”

Bhuvan’s phone buzzed before Shanta could offer twice. He read the examiner’s preliminary note beside the stove, where the blue biscuit tin had stood that morning. Now it was packed with the other evidence.

“The examination records an unidentified man,” he said. “Not Aadit Sood.”

“We know Aadit isn’t dead,” Shanta said.

“Now the examination says so too.” Bhuvan turned the screen toward Meher. “The dead man had an old forearm fracture. Healed years ago, from the look of it. We’ll record that for identification.”

Meher read the line twice. It gave her a bone, not a name.

“And the ring?” Aadit asked.

“The coat and ring tell us what was on him. They don’t tell us who wore them.” Bhuvan put his phone away. “Both stay with the evidence.”

Aadit looked at Meher’s untouched lunch. “Does this end the questions for her?”

“Her movements last night still need checking. But she said she couldn’t identify the face. I’m not going to treat her as the obvious killer of a husband standing here asking me questions.” Bhuvan capped his pen. “I need the dead man’s movements. I need to know how your things reached him. Who did you meet at the shed?”

“I can show you the pump room again.”

“You already have.”

Aadit drew his plate toward him, but didn’t sit. Bhuvan wrote down the refusal while Shanta took the cold dal away despite Meher’s protest.

At Anardana Dhaba, a driver at the far table asked for his change without looking up from Bhuvan’s uniform. Tara counted out three ten-rupee notes and slapped them into his palm.

“Tell them about Meher,” she said when he left. “They’ve been calling her the wife who burned her husband. One even asked if I’d seen her buy kerosene.”

“I’ll speak to the drivers. The dead man hasn’t been identified. If anyone knows a man who’s missing, I need an account, not a guess about a ring.”

Tara shut the till hard enough to rattle its coins. “You’ll get guesses anyway, ji. At least make them guess about the right man.”

Bhuvan watched her straighten a bent ten-rupee note. Years ago, after a theft, he had taken bus fare from his own wallet and pressed it into Manu’s hand outside this dhaba. Manu had asked first whether Tara had managed to open that morning. Then he’d asked Bhuvan not to tell her about the money; he wanted to tell her himself. When he hadn’t, Bhuvan had done it.

He bought a tea he didn’t want and left before Tara could ask why he was still watching the till.

Back at Cedar Terrace, Bhuvan laid a written request on the table: Aadit’s account of the meeting at the shed. Meher left it between them. She would not invent the name he refused to give.

Aadit glanced at the examiner’s note on Bhuvan’s phone. “Can I have a copy of the injury finding?”

Bhuvan covered the screen with his hand. “Tell me why.”

Aadit looked at Meher, then back at the line about the forearm. “Someone might recognize it.”

He still would not say who. Meher understood then that Aadit had a man in mind—and meant to find out if he was dead before telling her his name.

# A Sleeve in the Ash

Shanta tipped Aadit’s rajma into a pan and lit the stove. Meher’s plate sat on the table where Shanta had put it back, the dal still wearing its cold skin.

“Let her eat before you ask anything else,” Aadit told Bhuvan.

“I’d rather answer now,” Meher said.

Shanta stirred the rajma. “He is asking for ten minutes, beti.”

“For me?”

The spoon struck the pan once. Shanta lowered the flame beneath Aadit’s lunch.

Bhuvan opened the photograph on his phone and set it between Meher and the untouched plate. “Tell me what made you say this was his coat.”

Meher enlarged the left sleeve. Beneath the ash, a short line of pale stitches crossed the seam at an angle. “I mended that last week. The lining kept catching his watch.”

“You’re certain?”

“About my bad sewing? Yes. Not about the man inside it.” She pushed the phone back. “I said the ring looked like Aadit’s. I never recognized the face.”

Bhuvan wrote that down. “When did you last see the coat at home?”

“On the hook by the kitchen door. When I came back from Lower Bazaar, around nine-fifteen.”

He wrote *around*.

Shanta turned off the stove, though Aadit’s rajma had only begun to steam.

“At nine-forty,” Bhuvan said, “Shanta found your room empty.”

Meher looked at the hot-water bottle lying beside Shanta’s chair, its wool cover neatly folded over it. “I went out.”

Aadit’s hand settled on the back of her chair.

“Where?” Bhuvan asked.

“Toward the Bagh Number Seven turnoff. I was looking for Aadit. I turned back at the road; I didn’t go into the orchard.”

“You told me you stayed upstairs.”

“I thought I had until ten. Then Shanta gave you a time, and I still didn’t say where I’d gone.” Meher kept her hands flat beside the plate. “After the fire, ‘I went looking for my husband near the shed’ sounded rather convenient.”

Aadit reached across her for the plate. “She doesn’t have to guess minutes because you’ve asked her the same thing six times.”

Meher took the plate before he could lift it. “I’m not guessing where I walked.”

Bhuvan capped his pen. “I can check the route. I can ask whether anyone saw you. I can check the sleeve repair.” He tapped the photograph without turning it toward her again. “None of that tells me who dressed the dead man in this coat. The forearm fracture may help us learn his name; it doesn’t change what you saw last night.”

Shanta slid Aadit’s hot lunch onto the table. It displaced Bhuvan’s notebook by an inch.

“Your first statement stays,” Bhuvan told Meher. “Add this in your own words.”

He gave her a clean sheet. Meher wrote about the hook, the turnoff and the point at which she had turned back. At the bottom, before she signed, she looked at Aadit.

“Did you tell me you were going to the shed?”

“No.”

He said it quickly. Bhuvan waited, pen ready for whatever came next. Aadit drew his hot plate toward him and said nothing more.

Meher signed her route to the turnoff. The one man who could explain what had taken him to the shed sat beside her, alive, and let her account stand alone.

# Two Death Stories

“What date did my baby die?” Tara asked from the veranda step.

Shanta lifted two hot rotis from the tawa and put them beneath an upturned steel bowl beside Aadit’s plate. Only then did she turn around.

“You’ve come up from the dhaba. Have tea first.”

“I’ll have a date first.”

Aadit set down his spoon. Shanta glanced at him, then at the pickle jars along the veranda wall, as though one of them might need urgent attention.

“Come inside, Tara. There’s no need to do this where everyone can hear.”

“I need him to hear.”

Shanta’s hand remained on the steel bowl. “Thirteenth of January. Late afternoon. At the hospital.”

Tara had carried the question up the Walnut Steps without once deciding what she would do with an answer. Now she had one, neat enough to write on a form.

“Did you see him then?”

“You had just given birth. You weren’t fit to hear every detail at once.” Shanta reached for the sugar tin she kept on the veranda shelf. “One spoon or two?”

“Ma,” Aadit said, “did you see him?”

“I asked her.” Tara did not raise her voice. She didn’t need Aadit to make the question bigger; she needed Shanta to answer it.

Shanta loosened the tin’s lid, though nobody had accepted tea. “I’ve told you what they told us.”

That winter, when Tara had asked the same questions until even the dhaba drivers stopped ordering a second cup, Manu had brought her the old cash ledger. He’d ruled a line across an empty page with the edge of a bus ticket.

“Put it here,” he’d said. “Then you won’t have to trust either of us to remember.”

On the facing page, in his square handwriting, he had promised to put ₹600 back in her till by Friday. No later entry crossed out the debt. He had stolen from her and still sat beside her while she wrote down Shanta’s words. Tara had believed the date was one thing she could keep.

Aadit pushed his plate away. “I don’t know the date. I should have asked eight years ago, but I took what I was told.”

“And now?” Tara asked.

“I can speak to Ma alone. Find out exactly—”

“No.” She opened her cloth bag. “Whatever she tells you, she can tell me.”

Shanta put the sugar tin down. “The thirteenth. Late afternoon. The hospital should have a record.”

Tara took out the ledger. Its cover had a dark thumbprint near the spine, ground in by years of counting change. She found the page Manu had ruled and turned it toward Aadit.

Beneath the date Tara had written that winter were the words: *Shanta says he died at the hospital, 12 January, before sunrise.*

Aadit read them twice. Tara had been given two different deaths; the ledger could not tell her whether either had happened.

“Which date should I take to the hospital, Shanta ji?”

# The Records Window

“Five minutes,” Bhuvan said, holding up his written request.

Mrs. Dhamija pulled the records shutter down with a metal bang. The clock above it showed 4:37.

“Five minutes ago, Inspector.”

Meher looked at the sheet clipped behind his request: the preliminary finding on the burned man’s healed forearm fracture. “If the clinic treated him, we may get a name. Then we can ask who could put Aadit’s coat on him.”

“A name might tell us who broke an arm,” Bhuvan said. “First we find one.”

At the far end of reception, Naina spotted light beneath another hatch. She leaned over the counter. “Dhamija aunty? You’re still entering today’s receipts.”

Mrs. Dhamija’s face appeared behind the glass. “And that is why I am still here.”

“Could you look in the old injury index?”

“No. The records room is locked.”

“Could I—”

“No.”

Meher slid the request toward the invoice drop slot. Naina caught her wrist.

“That’s for bills. He’ll get a receipt saying the police paid for six boxes of gauze.”

“I’d like six boxes of answers,” Meher said, but she took the paper back.

Bhuvan uncapped his pen. “I’ll leave it for morning.”

“Wait.” Naina kept her eyes on Dhamija. “Is the reception inward register closed?”

Dhamija glanced at the clock. “Not yet.”

“A signed police request can go in that.”

“It can be received,” Dhamija said. “That doesn’t mean I open a single patient file tonight.”

Bhuvan checked the heading before he signed. *Search of injury registers for healed forearm fracture, approximate period attached.* Below it, he had specified that the finding could not identify the burned man. There was no patient name.

Meher noticed him read that last line twice. “Were you going to put one down?”

“No.” He set his pen against the paper without signing. “Years ago I gave Manu bus fare and promised he could tell Tara himself. I told her before his bus left.”

Meher knew Bhuvan had broken that promise. She hadn’t expected him to bring it up here, with Dhamija waiting behind the glass.

“On my way back, he was outside the dhaba,” Bhuvan said. “The side-gate latch had come loose. He wound a piece of wire around it while Tara counted the till inside. Didn’t knock. Didn’t thank me for the fare, either.”

“Did he catch the bus?”

“Yes.” Bhuvan signed the request. “I’d already decided what he’d do. I won’t write a name on this because I’ve decided it again.”

Dhamija opened the reception hatch by six inches. Bhuvan passed in the signed request with the finding attached. She numbered it in a ruled register, wrote the time and pressed a purple stamp onto his copy.

“No records have been searched,” she said, handing it back.

“I know.”

Meher read the stamp. At least nobody could say tomorrow that the request had never arrived.

Dhamija tapped a notice inside the hatch. The records window would open at eight-thirty. At nine, the older registers were being moved to clinic storage.

“You have my request,” Bhuvan said.

“I have received it. It goes into the correspondence queue.” Dhamija pointed to a second line on the notice. “If you want the injury index checked before it goes into a carton, come at opening with that stamped copy. Sign the urgent-search register here, in person. Then I can hold the index back and search it while you wait.”

“And if he’s late?” Naina asked.

“The porters take it at nine. After inventory, the cartons stay sealed for three weeks.”

Bhuvan folded the stamped copy once, carefully, and put it in his breast pocket. Meher watched him check the clock: 4:42. He had a name he refused to write, and until eight-thirty tomorrow, no record that could prove him wrong.
